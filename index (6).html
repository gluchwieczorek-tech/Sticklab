1 · The rule in Norway — with sources

Short version for parents: your child must wear a neck guard at both practice and games. It has been that way for kids and youth for a long time — and since 2024 it applies to everyone, seniors included.

  • The rule text: IIHF Official Rule Book Rule 9.12, adopted in Norway via NIHF Spilleregler (quoted at the top of this page): an approved neck laceration protector is mandatory, must cover as much of the neck as possible, be worn properly and not be modified. Enforcement: referee warning, then a minor penalty (2 min).
  • For the youngest: NIHF’s Kampreglement lists the neck guard as mandatory equipment in kids’ hockey too (current version revised by the federation board 23 Mar 2026, hockey.no).
  • The timeline: after Adam Johnson’s death (October 2023), the NIHF board decided on 30 Oct 2023 to extend the mandate to seniors (hockey.no news). The rule change came in March 2024 and applies from the 2024/25 season (Dagbladet, 2 Oct 2024, federation president Tage Pettersen). The IIHF did the same internationally: council decision December 2023, Rule 9.12 in the rulebook from 2024/25.
  • Also recommended: Rule 9.5 of the same rulebook strongly recommends cut-resistant socks and base layers for all players.

Sources with dates: hockey.no “Forbundsstyret vil endre regel om halsbeskytter” (31 Oct 2023) · NIHF Spilleregler ver. 30 Jul 2025 · NIHF Kampreglement rev. 23 Mar 2026 · Dagbladet “Norge tar grep” (2 Oct 2024) · iihf.com “IIHF mandates neck laceration protection at all levels” (Dec 2023). All checked 10 Jul 2026.

Boys and girls: collars are unisex, and so are most shirts. A dedicated girls fit in Yth/Jr comes from CCM FTW (in the catalog). Either way, check the fit — shirts follow clothing sizes.

Certifications — this is the actual approval:

  • BNQ (CAN/BNQ 9415-370): the Canadian neck protector standard since 1990 — required by Hockey Canada since 1993. It is being replaced by…
  • ISO 10256-5 (2017): the international standard built on the BNQ one; adopted in Canada as CAN/BNQ 0102-565. If you see a BNQ or ISO 10256-5 mark, the protector itself has been tested as a neck guard.
  • CE / UKCA: European/UK conformity marking for protective equipment. HECC: the US certification body, which launched its own neck protector program in January 2025.

Marketing vs. spec: “ANSI Level 3/6” and “A5” describe the fabric’s cut resistance under general material standards — useful for comparison, but not a neck guard approval. Look for the BNQ/ISO/CE mark first, and read the cut level as bonus information. The Norwegian rule requires “approved neck protection” without naming a single standard — certified models from established brands are the safe choice.

Sources: bnq.qc.ca (standard pages for 9415-370 and 0102-565/ISO 10256-5), hecc.org (15 Jan 2025), product pages at bauer.com and hockeystore.no. Checked 10 Jul 2026.

4 · The drying routine as a family habit

Wet gear rusts skate steel, smells, and wears out velcro and seams. The routine takes under ten minutes and needs no special rack:

  • Let the child own steps 1–2 from the very first season. That is half the point.
  • Neck guard and shirt: hang to air, wash regularly per the care label — sweat degrades the material over time, and a protector in good condition is part of the approval.
  • Wipe skate steel with a towel and never leave it in damp soakers overnight.
  • A dedicated drying rack is nice, not necessary. We found no hockey-specific racks at hockeystore.no (checked 10 Jul 2026) — an ordinary laundry rack works. Against odor, the store carries scent sprays (Odor-Aid and others).
